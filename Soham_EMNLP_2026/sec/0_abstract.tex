\begin{abstract}
Large language model agents increasingly browse the web on a user's
behalf, completing tasks such as job applications, purchases, and form
submissions. To do so without supervision they are granted the user's
personally identifiable information (PII)---names, addresses, payment
details, government identifiers---creating a confused-deputy attack
surface in which adversarial web content can coerce the agent into
forwarding that data to an attacker. Existing web-agent safety
benchmarks score proxy outcomes such as click compliance or trajectory
divergence rather than the terminal field-level submission of PII, and
none combine attacker-controlled sites, a structured PII profile, and
multi-turn conversational attacks.

We introduce \textsc{Scammer4U}, a pre-registered benchmark of 91
attacker-controlled environments and 10 benign-twin baselines spanning
16 site categories and 8 attack vectors, organised on an 8-axis
factorial taxonomy whose paired-sibling environments isolate the effect
of individual design factors. We evaluate four frontier agents (GPT-5
mini, Claude Haiku 4.5, Gemini 3 Flash, Llama 4 Scout) across four
escalating mitigation conditions, measuring PII leakage, attack
success, task completion, and an LLM-as-judge detection rate.

Frontier agents leak critical-tier PII at 54--93\% under no privacy
guidance. Our central finding is a detection--action gap: within the
pre-registered reflection condition, agents whose reasoning an
independent judge confirms has flagged the site as suspicious still
submit critical PII in 35.9\% of sessions, versus 66.1\% when no such
recognition is verbalised---a 30.2-point gap across all four models.
Prompt-level mitigation narrows leakage but is sharply model-dependent,
and verbalised suspicion does not reliably prevent submission. These
results motivate output-level defenses that do not depend on the agent
recognising an attack before acting.
\end{abstract}
