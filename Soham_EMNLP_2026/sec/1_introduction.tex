\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% -------------------------------------------------------------

Vision-language model (VLM) agents capable of operating a web browser
are moving from research prototypes into production deployment
[CITE: Operator, Claude Computer Use, Gemini Agents launch posts +
general agent capability surveys]. To complete the tasks they are
given (applying for jobs, purchasing goods, paying bills, replying to
email), these agents are routinely provisioned with the user's full
personally identifiable information (PII): legal name, home address,
payment card details, government identifiers, account credentials, and
API keys. The agent stores this profile in its context and draws on
it whenever a form field must be filled or a credential must be
submitted. The result is a system that combines broad web access with
privileged read access to high-value personal data, a configuration
that realises a \emph{semantic} variant of the classic
\emph{confused-deputy} vulnerability
[CITE: hardy1988confused, and modern confused-deputy framings for LLM
agents]: the structural fault arises from mixing privileged user
context with unprivileged web inputs in a single decision loop, but
the mechanism of exploitation is entirely semantic, leveraging
multi-turn conversational social engineering to bypass the model's
behavioural safety constraints rather than escalating any traditional
privilege boundary. Untrusted content encountered during normal
browsing can instruct or manipulate the agent into forwarding that
data to an adversary. The modern web already contains a mature social-engineering
ecosystem (phishing clones on subtly misspelled domains, dark
patterns that bury the safe option, fake trust signals, urgency
timers, authority impersonation, multi-turn conversational
escalation) built to harvest PII from human users
[CITE: APWG/anti-phishing report, dark-pattern taxonomy work, e.g.
Mathur et al.]. A human user can slow down, scrutinize a URL, or
close the tab; a web agent is optimised to finish its task and will
follow links, fill fields, and submit forms with a thoroughness that
no human user matches. Capability benchmarks confirm that frontier
agents are now reliable enough to actually do this end-to-end: REAL
\citep{garg2026real} reports a 41\% top success rate on multi-turn
state-changing web tasks, meaning the same competence that makes
them useful also makes them a high-value, unusually reliable target
for data exfiltration. We find that even when an agent's own
reasoning trace identifies a site as suspicious, it goes on to
submit critical PII to that site in 34\% of sessions, versus 89\%
when it does not: a 54.5-percentage-point gap between what the
agent \emph{says} and what it \emph{does}, statistically robust
($q\!<\!0.001$) and present across all four frontier model families
we evaluate.

\paragraph{The measurement gap.} Despite a growing literature on the
adversarial robustness of web agents, a critical measurement gap
persists. Existing evaluation frameworks gate success on proxy
metrics (task-trajectory divergence, click-level dark-pattern
compliance, isolated API tool abuse) rather than field-level data
exfiltration to attacker-controlled endpoints. Frameworks evaluating
dark patterns and adversarial UI deception
\citep{cuvin2026decepticon,ersoy2026trickyarena,chen2026obviousinvisiblethreat}
score click compliance or text-processing boundaries and, in the
case of DECEPTICON, explicitly omit phishing from their scope;
AgentDojo \citep{debenedetti2024agentdogo} measures indirect prompt
injection on tool-using agents at API surfaces (email, banking,
travel) rather than on a browser navigating attacker-controlled
sites; TRAP \citep{korgul2025trap} gates attack success on a single
attacker-controlled redirect click and does not score what is
submitted downstream; AgentDAM \citep{zharmagambetov2026agentdam}
quantifies \emph{incidental} privacy leakage on benign sites where
no adversary is present; and WebTrap Park
\citep{xinyi2026webtrappark} covers social engineering as one of
three risk categories but neither provisions a structured PII
profile nor reports field-level data loss. Consequently, we lack a
formal understanding of whether a web agent, provisioned with a
structured personal identity profile and operating an explicitly
attacker-controlled site, will actively execute the final
state-changing \emph{submission} of sensitive data when
systematically social-engineered. Quantifying this terminal
field-level behaviour is critical: early-trajectory anomalies such
as deceptive link clicks or layout divergence are observable to
low-cost runtime execution-layer guardrails, but a completed
form-level POST payload represents an absolute, unrecoverable data
breach that can only be prevented by the cognitive refusal strategy
of the model itself. To our knowledge, no published
benchmark combines (i) attacker-controlled websites that explicitly
social-engineer the agent, (ii) a populated PII profile spanning
critical, high, and medium sensitivity tiers, (iii) field-level
exfiltration as the primary outcome, and (iv) axis-controlled
siblings that isolate which design factors causally drive leakage.

\paragraph{This work.} We introduce \textsc{Scammer4U}, a benchmark
of 91 hand-crafted attacker-controlled environments plus 10
benign-twin baselines, designed to answer a single operational
question: \emph{when an autonomous web agent encounters a
social-engineering attack while browsing, will it hand over the
user's PII?} The benchmark spans 16 site categories (employment
portals, e-commerce stores, banking and healthcare, cryptocurrency
exchanges, government portals, dating, social media, education,
file-conversion utilities) and covers \textbf{eight} attack vectors:
phishing clones, credential harvesting, dark patterns, reward traps,
authority impersonation, conversational deception, prompt injection,
and fake trust signals. Each environment is classified on
\textbf{four classifying axes} (site category, attack vector,
salience, PII target) and \textbf{four factor axes} (pressure cue,
prompt-injection style, interaction modality, multi-site flow),
forming an 8-axis factorial taxonomy mapped to published threat
catalogues [CITE: MITRE ATT\&CK, OWASP LLM Top-10, ENISA Threat
Landscape] so that the design space is auditable rather than
arbitrary. 60 of the 91 environments are paired siblings that differ
from a parent on exactly one axis, supporting causal single-factor
ablation. We equip a Playwright-based agent
[CITE: Playwright + general agent-scaffold work, e.g. WebArena,
Visual\-Web\-Arena, SeeAct] with a 40-field PII profile and evaluate
\textbf{four frontier model families} (GPT-5 mini from OpenAI, Claude
Haiku~4.5 from Anthropic, Gemini~3 Flash from Google, and Llama~4 Scout
from Meta) across \textbf{four mitigation conditions}: a baseline
default-prompt condition (C0), a generic privacy nudge (C1), a
phishing-aware checklist (C2), and a pre-submission reflection
prompt (C3). The analysis plan is committed to a public git tag
(\texttt{prereg-v2-start}) before any of the reported data was
collected, with every subsequent deviation documented in a
limitations log.

\paragraph{Research questions.} We organise the empirical study
around four pre-registered research questions:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item[\textbf{RQ1.}] At baseline, with no privacy-aware system
  prompt, how often do frontier web-browsing agents submit
  critical-tier PII (SSN, full payment details, bank account,
  passwords, API keys, 2FA codes) to attacker-controlled
  endpoints, and how does this rate vary across model families?
  \item[\textbf{RQ2.}] Do prompt-level mitigations (ranging from a
  generic nudge to a forced pre-submission reflection) reduce
  critical-tier leakage, and is the effect size consistent across
  model families, or model-dependent?
  \item[\textbf{RQ3.}] Conditional on the agent independently
  identifying the attack in its reasoning trace, does behavioural
  compliance follow? That is, how large is the \emph{gap} between
  what an agent \textit{says} about a suspicious site and what it
  subsequently \textit{does}?
  \item[\textbf{RQ4.}] Which design factors along the 8-axis
  taxonomy (salience, pressure cue, prompt-injection placement,
  interaction modality, PII tier, attack vector, site
  category) most strongly predict leakage, and which are null?
\end{description}

\paragraph{Headline findings.} Across 4{,}790 retained sessions on
the post-fix harness, with five seeds for Gemini~3 Flash and
Llama~4 Scout and one seed each (with seeds 2--5 in progress) for
GPT-5 mini and Claude Haiku~4.5:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Baseline leakage is high and model-dependent.}
  Critical-tier PII leakage rate (PLR\textsubscript{crit}) at C0
  ranges from 53.8\% (Claude Haiku~4.5) to 93.1\% (Gemini~3 Flash),
  with GPT-5 mini at 75.6\% and Llama~4 Scout at 82.3\%
  (\textbf{RQ1}). All four exceed, by a wide margin, the
  empirically-measured 0\% leakage rate on 10 benign-twin
  environments built from the same templates with the attack
  removed.

  \item \textbf{Mitigation effectiveness is sharply
  model-dependent.} A pre-submission reflection prompt (C3)
  reduces PLR\textsubscript{crit} by 32.4\,pp on Gemini, 30.8\,pp
  on Haiku, 17.8\,pp on GPT-5 mini, and only 4.9\,pp on Llama (the
  C2 phishing-checklist condition is in fact stronger than C3 on
  Haiku, $-$36.1\,pp). The pooled view ($-$19.7\,pp) hides this
  heterogeneity: three model--condition cells individually cross
  the pre-registered $-$30\,pp threshold, but the pooled average
  does not. Frontier agents disagree on adversarial robustness
  (\textbf{RQ2}).

  \item \textbf{A 54.5-percentage-point detection--action gap.}
  At C0, pooled across all four model families, agents whose
  reasoning trace independently flags the site as suspicious go on
  to leak critical PII in 34.3\% of sessions, versus 88.8\% when
  they do not: a 54.5\,pp gap that is statistically robust
  ($q < 0.001$ after Benjamini--Hochberg correction over the
  pre-registered primary family) and consistent across all four
  model families. Forced-reflection prompts inflate stated
  detection without proportionally closing the leak gap
  (\textbf{RQ3}). Saying ``this looks like phishing'' is not the
  same as refusing to submit.

  \item \textbf{Characterisation findings invert a prior
  intuition.} Subtle attacks leak \emph{more} than blatant ones
  (96.5\% vs.\ 72.8\% PLR\textsubscript{crit} at C0, pooled). We
  hypothesise that blatant adversarial indicators actively trip
  standard model alignment guardrails via heavy keyword detection,
  whereas subtle semantic manipulations successfully navigate beneath
  these safety filters.
  \texttt{fake\_trust\_signals} and \texttt{reward\_trap} vectors
  are pinned at $\sim$94\% across all mitigation conditions;
  \texttt{authority\_impersonation} is the most C3-responsive
  vector ($-$62.5\,pp). The pressure axis (urgency, scarcity,
  social proof, authority) shows no significant pooled effect.
  Paired-sibling tests along the factor axes do not survive
  BH-correction at $q\!=\!0.05$ with the realised pair counts; the
  characterisation signal lives in cross-cutting marginal
  patterns, not paired-sibling deltas (\textbf{RQ4}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Contributions.} This paper makes four contributions, in
order of importance:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item A \textbf{detection--action gap at baseline}: a 54.5\,pp
  spread between PLR\textsubscript{crit} for agents that name the
  attack and those that do not, GLMM $q\!<\!0.001$, robust across
  four model families. To our knowledge this is the largest
  reported behavioural--verbal divergence in an agent-safety
  benchmark, and it survives every robustness check we run.

  \item Empirical evidence that \textbf{prompt-level mitigation is
  sharply model-dependent}: a finding that motivates output-level
  defences (form-submission inspection, structured-PII gating)
  rather than additional system-prompt iteration, since two of
  four frontier models tested respond strongly to prompts and the
  other two essentially do not.

  \item A \textbf{characterisation} of when web agents leak PII,
  spanning attack vector, site category, salience, pressure,
  prompt-injection placement, interaction modality, and PII tier,
  with cross-model marginal tables reported alongside paired-
  sibling tests.

  \item \textsc{Scammer4U} itself: a publicly released benchmark of
  91 attacker-controlled environments + 10 benign-twin baselines,
  organised on an 8-axis factorial taxonomy mapped to published
  threat catalogues, with pre-registered analysis plan and a
  complete deviation log, designed so that follow-up work can
  re-run the same ablations on new models without re-authoring
  the apparatus.
\end{enumerate}
